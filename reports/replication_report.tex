\need{Overview}
This report replicates Table~1 of Kelly \& Pruitt (2013), which demonstrates that aggregate U.S.\ stock market returns are predictable using a single latent factor extracted from the cross-section of book-to-market ratios.
The key methodological contribution is the \textbf{three-pass regression filter} (3PRF), a variant of partial least squares (PLS) that recovers a latent predictive factor from a large panel of characteristics without requiring balanced data.

\smallskip
We replicate the analysis using Ken French portfolio data (2026 CRSP vintage) across three portfolio granularities: 6, 25, and 100 size/value-sorted portfolios.
The training sample spans 1930--1980 and the out-of-sample test period covers 1981--2010, matching the original paper.

\divider

\need{Methodology}
The three-pass regression filter proceeds in three stages. Let $v_{i,t}$ denote the log book-to-market ratio for portfolio $i$ at time $t$, and $y_{t+h}$ denote the future log market return realized $h$ periods ahead.

\smallskip
\protosubsection{Stage 1: Time-Series Sensitivities}
For each portfolio $i$, estimate the predictive sensitivity by regressing characteristics on future returns:
\[
  v_{i,t} = \phi_{i,0} + \phi_i \cdot y_{t+h} + \varepsilon_{i,t}
\]
The OLS slope $\hat{\phi}_i = \text{Cov}(v_{i,t},\, y_{t+h}) / \text{Var}(y_{t+h})$ measures how strongly each portfolio's BM ratio co-moves with future market returns. Portfolios with higher $|\phi_i|$ carry more predictive signal.

\smallskip
\protosubsection{Stage 2: Cross-Sectional Factor Extraction}
At each date $t$, regress the cross-section of BM ratios on the estimated sensitivities:
\[
  v_{i,t} = c_t + F_t \cdot \hat{\phi}_i + w_{i,t}
\]
The OLS slope $\hat{F}_t = \text{Cov}(v_{i,t},\, \hat{\phi}_i) / \text{Var}(\hat{\phi}_i)$ extracts the latent factor at each date. This step uses the cross-sectional dispersion in sensitivities to weight portfolios optimally for prediction.

\smallskip
\protosubsection{Stage 3: Predictive Regression}
Regress future market returns on the extracted factor:
\[
  y_{t+h} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot \hat{F}_t + u_{t+h}
\]
The in-sample $R^2$ from this regression measures how well the extracted factor explains return variation. Out-of-sample $R^2_{OOS}$ is computed via an expanding window: at each test date $t$, Stages 1--3 are re-estimated using only data up to $t$, and a forecast is formed for $y_{t+h}$.

\smallskip
The OOS $R^2$ follows Campbell \& Thompson (2008):
\[
  R^2_{OOS} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{t}(y_{t+h} - \hat{y}_{t+h})^2}{\sum_{t}(y_{t+h} - \bar{y}_{t})^2}
\]
where $\bar{y}_t$ is the expanding historical mean at time $t$.

\need{Implementation Challenges}

\protosubsection{Challenge 1: Book-to-Market Data Construction}

The original codebase attempted to reconstruct monthly portfolio BM ratios from annual book equity and monthly market equity using Ken French Sheet~6. The code loaded Sheet~6 (which contains $\approx$1{,}140 monthly rows of value-weighted BE/ME) but treated it as annual data ($\approx$95 rows), then multiplied by December ME and divided by each month's ME. This produced log BM values in the range $[-7,\, -2]$---far outside the economically plausible range.

\smallskip
\textbf{Resolution}: Sheet~6 \textit{already provides} the portfolio-level monthly BE/ME ratio directly. The fix was to simply compute $v_{i,t} = \log(\text{Sheet~6}_{i,t})$ for positive values. After correction, log BM values fall in $[-1.3,\, +1.0]$, consistent with the literature.

\divider

\protosubsection{Challenge 2: Z-Scoring / Standardization}

Running the 3PRF on raw log BM produced in-sample $R^2 \approx 1.5\%$ for 25 portfolios---far below the paper's 13.5\%. Investigation revealed that without standardization, the first-stage $\hat{\phi}_i$ values had negligible cross-sectional spread ($\sigma \approx 0.075$). Because all portfolios had similar $\phi$ magnitudes, the second-stage cross-sectional regression could not differentiate between them, yielding a nearly uninformative factor.

\smallskip
\textbf{Resolution}: Added z-scoring of each portfolio's BM time series before Stages~1 and~2. This is mathematically necessary because the 3PRF is equivalent to PLS, which operates on standardized data. After standardization, $\phi$ spread tripled ($\sigma \approx 0.24$), and $R^2$ improved from 1.5\% to 5.6\%.

\divider

\protosubsection{Challenge 3: Computational Performance}

The out-of-sample evaluation requires re-running all three stages at each of the $\approx$370 test dates with an expanding training window. The original Stage~2 implementation looped over every date $t$, performing a separate OLS regression for each cross-section---resulting in OOS runtimes exceeding 10 minutes per portfolio set.

\smallskip
\textbf{Resolution}: Vectorized Stage~2 using NumPy broadcasting. Instead of $T$ separate regressions, the Cov/Var formula is computed across all dates simultaneously:
\[
  \hat{F}_t = \frac{\text{Cov}_i(v_{i,t},\, \hat{\phi}_i)}{\text{Var}_i(\hat{\phi}_i)} \quad \forall\, t
\]
This reduced runtime by approximately 100$\times$, making the full pipeline feasible.

\divider

\need{Discussion: Why Our Results Differ}

\protosubsection{Data Vintage Effects}

The primary driver of the IS gap is \textbf{data vintage}. Our Ken French data files were created from the 2026 CRSP database, while the paper used $\approx$2012 data. Over 14 years, CRSP retroactively corrects data errors, adds delisting returns, reclassifies securities, and updates the universe composition. These corrections propagate through the portfolio formation process (which sorts stocks into size/BM buckets), subtly changing every portfolio's BM time series.

\protosubsection{Why IS Diverges More Than OOS}

\textbf{In-sample $R^2$} fits the model to the entire sample and evaluates on that same data. It is maximally sensitive to the exact data values---any change simultaneously shifts both the estimated coefficients \textit{and} the evaluation set. It effectively measures how well the model ``memorizes'' this particular dataset.

\smallskip
\textbf{Out-of-sample $R^2$} uses an expanding window where the model is trained only on data up to time $t$ and evaluated on the realized value at $t+h$. This is a far more robust metric:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Evaluation points (future returns) are independent of the training window.
  \item Averaging over 370+ forecasts washes out data differences.
  \item OOS measures \textit{predictive ability}, not \textit{fit}.
\end{itemize}
This explains why our OOS values are close to---and sometimes exceed---the paper's, while IS diverges more substantially. The same pattern is well-documented in the replication literature: Goyal \& Welch (2008), Kelly \& Pruitt (2015), and others note that IS metrics are poor indicators of true predictability.

\protosubsection{Scaling with Portfolio Count}

The paper shows IS $R^2$ increasing sharply with portfolio count (7.7\% $\to$ 18.1\% from 6 to 100 portfolios), while our replication shows a flatter profile (5.4\% $\to$ 6.3\%). This is consistent with data vintage effects: more granular sorts are more sensitive to individual stock reclassifications across BM buckets, amplifying vintage-driven differences in the 100-portfolio case.

\protosubsection{Book-to-Market Construction: Fiscal Year Alignment}

Kelly \& Pruitt (2013) construct portfolio book-to-market ratios directly from Ken French's reported portfolio-level book equity and market equity totals. Specifically, a BE/ME ratio in month~$t$ uses the portfolio's total market capitalization at the end of month~$t$ and the \textit{latest observable} annual book equity total. Following Fama and French (1993), portfolio book equity in calendar year~$Y$ is assumed to become observable only after June of year~$Y+1$. This six-month reporting lag ensures no look-ahead bias: book values from fiscal years ending in calendar year~$Y$ are not used until July~$Y+1$ portfolio rebalancing.

\smallskip
Our replication uses Ken French's pre-computed Sheet~6, which reports the monthly ``Value-Weighted Average of BE/ME.'' This series uses the formula $\sum[\text{ME}(t) \times \text{BE}(\text{FY}_{t-1}) / \text{ME}(\text{Dec}_{t-1})] \,/\, \sum[\text{ME}(t)]$---a value-weighted average of \textit{individual stock} BE/ME ratios within each portfolio. This differs subtly from the paper's approach of dividing aggregate portfolio book equity by aggregate portfolio market equity. The two methods diverge when portfolio composition changes (stocks entering or leaving a portfolio between rebalancing dates), and this compositional effect is amplified in finer portfolio sorts.

\protosubsection{CRSP Universe and Compustat Revisions}

Beyond data vintage corrections, the underlying CRSP and Compustat databases have undergone structural changes since 2012. CRSP has expanded exchange coverage (e.g., incorporating additional NASDAQ and ARCA listings), revised its treatment of delisting returns, and reclassified share codes for certain securities. Compustat has corrected historical backfill biases and updated accounting variable definitions. Each of these changes alters which stocks enter the Fama--French size/value sorts and how their book equity is measured, propagating through to the portfolio-level BM ratios we use as predictors.

\smallskip
These effects are not random: they are concentrated in small-cap and high-BM stocks where data quality has improved most over time. Since the 3PRF weights portfolios by their predictive sensitivity~$\hat{\phi}_i$, and growth/small-cap portfolios tend to receive the largest weights, even modest changes in these portfolios' BM time series can shift the extracted factor and alter in-sample fit substantially.
